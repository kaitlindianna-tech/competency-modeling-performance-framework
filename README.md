# Competency Modeling & Performance Framework
## Translating Organizational Priorities Into Observable Performance Expectations

**Competency Modeling | Performance Management | Project Lead | 2025**

## Overview

What does "good performance" actually look like?

I served as project lead for a graduate I-O Psychology consulting project conducted in partnership with Rock City Enterprises to develop a competency-based performance framework. The project was designed as a pilot: establish core competencies with organization-wide applicability, then use the organization's food teams to develop and validate increasingly role-specific expectations.

The resulting framework translated competencies into observable behaviors across four job levels—Frontline Partner, Team Leader, Manager, and Director—and connected those behaviors to a five-point performance rating scale.

Beyond defining competencies, the project addressed a larger performance-management challenge: giving leaders a shared, behaviorally grounded framework for evaluating performance consistently.

## The Challenge

Rock City Enterprises was exploring changes to its organizational values, mission, and leadership framework while also considering opportunities to strengthen career progression and performance management.

This created an opportunity to use competency modeling as a practical starting point.

Rather than attempting to build a complete organization-wide model at once, the project established an architecture that could scale over time: core competencies applicable across the organization, followed by job-family and role-specific competencies that reflected the realities of different types and levels of work.

The organization's food teams were selected as the pilot population, providing a manageable starting point while spanning roles from frontline employees through Directors.

My responsibilities included:

## My Approach

The model was developed through a collaborative, iterative process rather than designed by the project team in isolation.

### 1. Establish a Starting Framework

The project team developed preliminary competencies based on our understanding of the organization's culture, existing expectations, business context, and job requirements.

These were intentionally treated as a starting point rather than a finished solution.

### 2. Co-Create With Organizational SMEs

Three SME groups represented different levels of the organization:

- C-suite leadership
- Directors
- Managers

The preliminary framework was brought into facilitated working sessions where SMEs discussed, challenged, revised, and refined the competencies in real time.

This allowed the framework to incorporate both strategic leadership perspectives and the operational realities experienced by leaders closer to frontline work.

### 3. Validate the Framework

Following the first round of SME sessions, validation surveys were used to evaluate the resulting competencies and essential functions.

The project team then developed preliminary behavioral anchors and returned to the SME groups for a second round of working sessions.

During these sessions, participants reviewed the behavioral expectations for clarity, relevance, scope, accuracy, and appropriate performance level.

A second validation survey was then used to evaluate the finalized behavioral anchors.

## The Solution

The final framework connected organizational expectations to progressively more specific performance requirements.

**Organizational Priorities → Core Competencies → Job-Family Competencies → Role-Specific Expectations → Behavioral Anchors → Performance Evaluation**

The pilot covered four organizational levels:

**Frontline Partner → Team Leader → Manager → Director**

This structure was designed so that the core framework could eventually remain consistent across the organization while additional job-family and job-specific competencies could be developed as the model expanded.

## Behaviorally Anchored Performance

A central goal of the project was to move performance evaluation away from vague impressions and toward observable behavior.

Each competency was translated into behavioral expectations using a five-point performance scale.

Three performance milestones were explicitly defined:

**1 — Does Not Meet Expectations**  
Observable behaviors indicating that performance falls below the requirements of the role.

**3 — Solid Performer**  
Observable behaviors representing consistent, successful performance at the current job level.

**5 — Role Model**  
Observable behaviors representing performance beyond the normal expectations of the current role.

Ratings of 2 and 4 were intentionally available for performance that fell between these defined behavioral milestones.

This structure gave evaluators concrete reference points while preserving enough flexibility to represent performance that did not fall neatly into a single category.

## Designing for Career Progression

The behavioral anchors were also designed to demonstrate how expectations increase as employees move through the organization.

For competencies spanning multiple job levels, the framework used a progression principle in which exceptional performance at one level intentionally began to resemble successful performance at the next.

For example:

**Frontline Partner — Level 5 → Team Leader — Level 3**

**Team Leader — Level 5 → Manager — Level 3**

**Manager — Level 5 → Director — Level 3**

This created a visible behavioral pathway between roles.

Rather than simply telling employees that advancement required "leadership potential" or stronger performance, the framework could show how the scope and complexity of expected behaviors changed as responsibility increased.

## Supporting Consistent Evaluation

Creating behavioral anchors was only one part of the performance-management solution.

The project also included practical guidance to help leaders use the framework consistently, including:

- Competency-based evaluation forms
- Guidance for documenting observable performance
- Behavior-focused note-taking practices
- Common performance-rating errors and strategies for reducing them
- Calibration recommendations
- Scenario-based evaluator practice
- Guidance for connecting observed behavior to competency ratings

The intent was to help evaluators answer not simply:

**"What rating do I think this employee deserves?"**

but:

**"What behaviors have I actually observed, and how do they compare with the expectations defined for this role?"**

## Outcome

The original competency labels developed through the graduate project were not implemented wholesale.

Following the project, Rock City Enterprises completed additional organizational work around its mission and values. The HR Director subsequently used the organization's newly established values as the foundation for its core competencies while retaining the behaviorally anchored performance approach informed by this project.

The resulting performance framework continued the central principle developed through the project: **performance expectations should be explicitly defined through observable behavior rather than left to individual interpretation.**

For me, that is the most meaningful outcome of the work.

The project did not simply produce an academic competency model. The process introduced an approach to defining performance expectations that the organization was able to adapt and use within its evolving performance-management system.

## What I Would Do Differently

With additional time and resources, I would begin with a more extensive discovery phase before developing the preliminary competency set.

This would include interviews with organizational leaders focused specifically on the future direction of the business, evolving workforce needs, and the capabilities they believed would become increasingly important. That discovery would provide a stronger strategic baseline for the competencies initially brought into the co-creation process.

I would also design implementation and training alongside the competency framework from the beginning.

Developing clear behavioral expectations is only valuable if leaders understand how to apply them consistently. A full implementation would therefore include a structured rollout and evaluator training focused on using behavioral anchors, documenting observable performance, recognizing common rating errors, and calibrating ratings across leaders and teams.
